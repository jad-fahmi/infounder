# Principles & evidence — the foundation under every mode

Load this when you need to justify a stance, settle a conflict between pieces of advice, or calibrate to a particular founder.

---

## 1. "Idea first, research later" — what it means, precisely

**It means:** generate and shape a concrete idea (with a hook and a mechanic) *before* you gate it behind research; let the idea morph through contact with real people; treat the first idea as a hypothesis that is "almost certainly wrong from the jump" and will be corrected by doing.

**It does not mean:** never validate. Validation is *mandatory* — it just happens by **selling, shipping and talking to people who have the problem**, not by desk analysis. "Idea first" is about **ordering**: bet → test → refine, not survey → decide → build.

### What the source material actually says
- **Perfect first picks are impossible.** Picking anything starts the feedback loop. The goal is your *best bad idea* as fast as possible, then make it less wrong. The belief that you must pick right on the first try "assumes that you, with zero context, having not talked to customers, are going to pick it right." (Hormozi)
- **You can only figure out what to work on by making contact with reality.** The perfect idea is "impossible to figure out in the abstract." Pick something you're curious about, commit, go deep, talk to customers; real usage "produces concrete data that will complement your abstract knowledge." (YC)
- **Launch to find out.** "Typically the only way to know for sure if your startup idea is good is to just launch it and find out." Your initial idea only needs enough interesting qualities to *morph in the right direction*. (YC, after Paul Graham)
- **Market research is part of the over-planning that kills startups.** Six months of pitch deck, financial model, market research and business plan kills more startups than bad ideas, by their account. A perfect plan is a plan for never starting. (Maurya)
- **Desk market analysis is "valid but not useful" before you have customers.** Investor tools (market sizing, comps, trend decks, exit slides) answer later-stage questions; the VC toolbox has no answer to "how do I get customer one?" Most successful companies looked, to investors, like off-trend, bad-model, bad-space, too-small ideas. (YC)
- **Don't spend $250k on studies when a $1,000 live test answers it.** Run your best promo in five candidate markets, compare cost per lead, open where it's lowest. Never ask opinion questions that a spreadsheet or a test can answer. (Hormozi)
- **Pricing is discovered, not researched.** Pick a number out of thin air and start charging tonight. (YC)
- **Turn the water on.** "If you have a house full of pipes you can spend ages checking every pipe, or you can turn the water on and you'll know exactly the work to be done." Ship, see what leaks, fix, repeat. (YC)
- **Start from people like you.** "People like you" as your who is "the quick and dirty way" — you already know their pains, so you skip research; and it makes you a missionary rather than a mercenary. (Hormozi)
- **But the idea still has to be *noticed*, not forced.** ~70% of the top-100 YC companies found their idea organically (from a job, a problem, a change in the world); people who sit down and brainstorm "tend to think of bad ones" — especially tar pits. Don't start a company just to start one: "wait to have a good idea… otherwise you'll be under pressure to make something up." (YC, Altman). *Reconciliation:* the idea should come from **observation and conviction** (your life, your domain, what changed), not from a research project — and then it gets tested by action.

### The five-minute sanity pass (allowed before building)
Cheap, fast, and *not* market research:
1. **Tar pit?** Google it. Is this an idea that has hit YC batch after batch for years (events-with-friends app, "Uber for X")? If so: who tried, why did they fail, what's the hard part — and do *you* have an insight they lacked?
2. **Why now?** What recently became possible (new tech/API/cost curve) or necessary (regulation, behavior shift, a new class of customer)?
3. **Proxy?** Is there a large, similar, non-competing company that proves the model (DoorDash proving delivery in a region it hadn't reached)?
4. **Who's tried & what did *all* competitors miss?** (Dropbox was the ~20th cloud-storage company; everyone else made you upload files by hand.) If there are *no* competitors, find out why — "often it's because no one wants the product."
5. **Can I reach the buyer?** Where do they actually spend time? (See `launch.md`.)

### What counts as real research (in order of value)
1. Selling a demo / pre-order (Maurya's *Demo → Sell → Build*)
2. Charging the first real money
3. A free pilot **with skin in the game** (homework or it gets billed; must yield feedback + testimonial + referral)
4. Conversations with people who *have the problem* (not startup experts) — specific, behavioral questions
5. Immersion in an idea space (the AtoB founders drove to truck stops and talked to truckers; talk to founders already in the space)
6. Cheap live tests (price, headline/story tests, $1,000 market test, fake-door landing page)
7. Expert chains: find 5 experts → ask each for their 5 best people → map the ecosystem → distill (a rapid-learning method for a new domain)
8. Shipping and watching real usage

### When to be more careful than "just ship"
- **Capital-heavy / regulated / safety-critical / hardware** — upfront structural homework (vehicle fit, capital need, regulation) is warranted; mistakes are expensive and slow to reverse.
- **Reliability-critical products** (email, payments, health) — people expect them to always work; turn the water on, but protect the core path.
- **Irreversible commitments** — money from friends & family, equity grants, legal structures, long leases.

## 2. The evidence ladder

| Rung | Signal | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Your own belief / a founder's conviction | hypothesis only |
| 1 | Advice from startup experts, investors, friends | low — they often don't have the problem; investors have incentives |
| 2 | Compliments, "I'd use that", survey answers, testimonials from *free* users | near zero |
| 3 | Real usage; return visits; they ask when the next version ships | moderate |
| 4 | Pre-order, deposit, signed LOI *with money*, paid pilot | strong |
| 5 | Repeat purchase, expansion, unprompted referrals | strongest |

**Say vs. do.** Bad validation questions (everyone says yes because it costs nothing): "Would you use this?" "Would you pay for this?" Better: "Will you pre-order it?" "Will you give me 30 minutes next Tuesday to set it up?" "Who else should I talk to — will you intro me today?" "If you don't do your homework I'll bill you."

**Fake-traction checklist** (flag these hard): a big-name "customer" that pays $0 · a free pilot where the champion has spent no political or financial capital · LOIs collected for fundraising while you know the software isn't used · thousands of free users, nobody paying (freemium trap) · glowing testimonials from people who'd never pay · investor interest · "3 startups will talk to me" mistaken for demand · a spike from a launch with no retention.

**The three-seconds test.** The best feedback you'll ever get is the 3 seconds after you tell someone the price. Watch their face.

**Separate who is talking.** *Experts* (startup people, mentors, VCs) give advice that "sounds very expert" but often don't have the problem; *users* can't tell you if it's a billion-dollar company but can instantly tell you whether they have the problem and what a better version looks like. Founders drift to experts because it's ten times easier. Investors are playing a different game (referral flow, firm politics) — their feedback is conflicted. Discount a failed founder's *opinion* of a market; keep their *facts*.

## 3. Calibrating to the founder

### Bringing expertise vs. building expertise (the first triage)
- **Bringing expertise** (10 years in mortgage underwriting; built an internal tool others still use; ex-real-estate agent): trust your gut more than you think. Test: *"Make something that would impress you / that you'd be proud of. Would you have bought it? Would you have replied to this email?"* Failure mode: the fearful expert who abandons their real insight to chase a fashion ("build AI because VCs fund AI").
- **Building expertise:** admit it. Pick a general area you're excited to learn, keep the MVP small, plan simple, hold ideas loosely ("this is what I'm thinking but I'm open-minded"). Failure mode: the overconfident novice who writes a two-year hiring plan and quotes market-size reports.
- **Weak-gut signals:** few opinions about what to work on; opinions identical to the 50th percentile; jobs stayed high-level; few jobs.
- **Don't weaponize founder-market fit against yourself** either: you don't need a decade of experience. "If you pick an idea you're curious about, go extremely deep and talk to customers, it's often possible to build extraordinary knowledge in a short time." (Boom Supersonic's founder came from ad-tech.) The bar: *"Could you actually run your customer's business? Could you teach a class on this problem?"*

### The 3 Ps (where ideas come from — you need only one)
**Pain** (a problem you personally overcame), **Profession** (a skill from work — decompose any business into "jobs to be done" and sell one fractional job), **Passion** (what you consume voluntarily). Pain + passion are less proven but higher upside; all three together and you're "set". Being the prospect beats researching the prospect.

### Goal size sets the path
"The bigger the goal, the narrower the scope of the path." A $10M outcome: almost any business. $100M: almost any business on a long enough timeline. Trillions: some form of tech/AI. Ask what outcome they actually want (income, cash flow, enterprise value, billions) before judging an idea — see vehicle fit in `monetization.md`.

### Step one before step two
Most "mere mortals" can't skip to step two (a lab, hard tech, a $100M raise) even though it sounds cooler. Sam Altman started with Loopt, a normal startup with a normal outcome, and the network it built enabled OpenAI; Musk started with Zip2 and PayPal-era web ideas to fund space; Thiel says "skip college" but went to Stanford, law school and a big firm. **Famous founders give end-state advice, not start-state advice.** Look at what they *did* first. Ask: *"What would I tell my own kid?"* Find the founder's real advantages (network, wealth, track record, expertise) and let those set how big a swing is sensible. ~80% of advice is identical for everyone; ~20% is personal.

### Advantages first-time founders really have
Nobody to impress (so they can pick dumb-sounding ideas — Airbnb, Coinbase — and take bigger risks); they talk to users instead of experts; no "autopilot" (reputation, press, friendly beta users) hiding a weak product; constraints that force a genuinely good product; honest feedback from people who don't care about you. Repeat-founder edge exists mainly in physical/capital-heavy businesses and lending, and in re-entering a domain you're not sick of. Beware "grass is greener": serial founders often wish they had first-timer advantages.

### Missionary vs. mercenary
People who survey trends and pick a category because "the market is growing, timing is right" are mercenaries; people who lived the problem tend to make the most money and last longer. Investors back obsession and domain detail (the investor who backed a man who knew the cost of every kernel of popcorn). Story first, logic second.

## 4. The idea as a hypothesis (and a node)

- **Idea space** (one level of abstraction above an idea): software for hospitals, fintech infrastructure, food delivery. Hit rates differ by orders of magnitude between spaces and flip over time. Choose a fertile space *where you have fit*, then iterate against real buyers; even a wrong first idea leaves adjacent good ideas to drift into (Fivetran got rejected twice, learned each time, found the right one).
- **Minimum *evolvable* product.** An early product is an amoeba exposed to the pressure of early adopters; they steer what it becomes. Tesla's first car was a $150,000 impractical roadster — it found early adopters who cared about acceleration and tech over comfort, and the lineage still shows. Design it to survive contact with users and adapt, not to be perfect.
- **Going deep is a way to find the better idea underneath**, not just to validate the one you began with. Surface pain → structural problem. (GovDash pivoted five times, renaming the company each time; the fifth, built on real procurement expertise, worked.)
- **Lottery ticket.** Every founder thinks they hold the winning number for a draw that already happened — so pitch with humility ("here's everything, it's probably all wrong, worth a shot"), not an NDA. Ideas are not the scarce asset; execution, endurance and focus are.
- **Burn the boats.** Several equally attractive ideas → pick one and go deep; parallel ideas produce bad data. Commitment is the elimination of alternatives. (Explore *within* a space sequentially, one committed idea at a time.)

## 5. Conflict rulings (where the sources pull in different directions)

| Tension | Ruling |
|---|---|
| "Your idea doesn't matter, your model does" (Maurya) vs. "Wait for a good, original idea" (Altman) | Both treat the idea as a hypothesis. Require a real insight (own problem + why-now + originality) *and* a sellable model. Don't wait for perfect; don't start from nothing. |
| Founder-market fit is #1 (YC list) vs. "don't gate yourself" (YC "go deep") | Fit is a *prior*; depth is a *route to acquiring fit*. If fit is thin, say so and prescribe the immersion plan. |
| Pick one idea (burn boats) vs. explore a space | Sequential: explore inside a space, commit to one idea at a time with a time-box. |
| Persist and iterate vs. "the vehicle matters more than effort" | Persist **inside a good vehicle**. If the vehicle is capped or a labor-heavy commodity, iteration alone won't fix it. Check the vehicle before the long push. |
| Think big vs. start tractable | Specific wedge, ambitious end state. Cost of ambition ≈ cost of modesty in effort — but don't skip step one. |
| Demo → sell → build (Maurya) vs. prototype with AI then find 10 users (Khan) | Sell the demo first if you can fake/mock it; if AI makes a working MVP cheaper than a demo, build it. Either way: no long build before real people. |
| Solo vs. co-founder | Prefer 2–3 founders; solo is viable but the psychological toll is underestimated. Never add a co-founder to please investors (it "ends in heartache 100% of the time"); never stop looking. Better none than a bad one. Trial a co-founder on a ~3-month project first. |
| Charge early vs. free models | Charge early by default. Free is legitimate only as open-core, *structured* freemium (a real upgrade constraint + tracked conversion), or a huge ad-supported consumer play — with a stated plan. |
| Sell to startups vs. enterprises | Only if you're in one of three proven games (see `monetization.md`). "Willing to talk" ≠ "easier to sell". |
| Focus (one thing) vs. optionality (a menu of 40 businesses) | Use menus to *ideate*; use focus to *decide*. |
| Fast money when broke vs. hurdles later | Both valid; depends on goal and starting capital. Ask. |

## 6. What infounder must NOT do (self-check)

- Think like an investor at idea stage (TAM, comps, exit slide, "venture-scale?").
- Answer a question with questions. Bring a bet.
- Give generic advice that could be pasted onto any startup. Cite their repo, their numbers, their words.
- Parrot a famous founder's end-state advice to a beginner.
- Optimize for how the idea *sounds* (dinner-party filter, Twitter-trend filter).
- Kill an idea for being small, boring, hard, or off-trend.
- Hype. A co-founder who calls everything "huge" is useless; calibrated enthusiasm is the product.
- Pretend certainty. Label assumptions and inferences; say what evidence would change your mind.
- Recommend scale-up moves (hiring, ads, PR, scalable infra, expansion) before pull exists.
