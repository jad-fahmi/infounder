# money — business model, offer, pricing, vehicle, funding path

Goal: turn an idea into a **one-page money model** — who pays, for what outcome, how much, when the first dollar arrives, whether the economics and the vehicle fit the founder's goal, and how it's funded — plus pricing experiments to run this week.

Work in this order. Skipping ahead (to funding, to tiers) before you know who pays and why is the classic error.

---

## 1. Who pays, and for what outcome

- **Sell the outcome, not the service.** "You don't want to sell tutoring; you want to sell the outcome of tutoring" — a test score, a better school, a better job. People want the hole, not the drill.
- **Anchor on the value delta**, not your cost or time. A student who attends a better school may earn ~$100k/year more, so $35/session is cheap. A $1M skill vs. a $50k salary costs $950k a year not to have. Price against the Delta.
- **Specific buyer, specific payer.** Who has the budget? Is the payer the user? Is the money cost-inelastic (insurance, mandated spend, "found money", someone else's money)?
- **Does the buyer change as customers grow?** (Founder → marketing team → procurement.) If the product only suits the early buyer, expect a wall.
- **Does the category scale with people or revenue?** People-scaled categories (HR, CRM) churn as the customer grows; revenue/activity-scaled ones (payments) stay and grow with them.
- **B2B/prosumer vs. consumer.** Most people spend little on consumer apps (personal software budgets are small); AI inference costs make consumer subscriptions hard; so many AI founders start with prosumers, businesses, or users with high ad value (doctors). Consumer companies still get built — via ads or huge scale.
- **Sell the outcome, own the stack (AI era).** Software cost → ~0; value migrates to customer trust, licenses, regulatory permission, outcome ownership. "Don't build software for insurance companies — be the insurer"; "don't build back-office software for banks — be the bank." (Illustrative: some AI-era startups buy a licensed carrier to own underwriting-to-service. Verify specifics before citing.) Full-stack = own all the economics with a fraction of the headcount.

## 2. The offer

**Value equation (Hormozi):** the perfect offer is incredibly **easy**, **guaranteed**, **immediate**, and **exactly what they want**. Build it from two lists — and motivation has only two levers: *give them more good stuff, or take away bad stuff.*

- **How part 1 — the good stuff:** easier, more certain, faster, exactly their way; and how the dream scenario *feels*. (Illustrative: a gym owner would pay for "fill my gym fast, guaranteed" when one month of new members rivals what they take home in a year. Use your own numbers.)
- **How part 2 — the bad stuff avoided:** the risk, the slowness, the effort, plus the **two sides of "hard"**: good things they normally have to *give up* (Taco Tuesday, time with family) and bad things they normally have to *start doing* (waking up early, working out). "What makes things hard is doing things we don't like and giving up things we like."
- **Get the lists** from yourself if you're the customer; otherwise interview (script in `launch.md`).
- **Combine:** *I help [who: 3+ of age/gender/profession/pain/interest] get [good stuff] without [bad stuff].* Examples: "…45-year-old women who just had kids get back into their high-school jeans without giving up time with their family"; "…non-fiction authors hit the WSJ list without looking cringe."
- **Name a unique mechanism** (when 10–100 others serve the niche): a checklist or sequence you wrap and name. First try going narrower in who/problem.
- **Niching raises price.** Illustrative ladder (made-up numbers): generic time-management PDF → for salespeople → for outbound salespeople → a system for garden and power-tool sellers. Each step up in specificity supports a much higher price, because a very specific buyer knows a few sales repay it and has almost no competition.
- **Make the customer's job-to-be-done bigger than your feature:** don't sell a better camera, sell a better photographer.

## 3. Price

- **Charge from day one.** Early adopters with a burning need are rarely price-sensitive; revenue in the bank is the only validation that can't be faked; paying customers give sharper feedback than free users ever will ("an angry customer paying a lot is worth more than a nobody who isn't willing to pay"). Not charging B2B customers is an anti-signal — they wonder if you'll support them.
- **Pick a number out of thin air and start tonight.** Dropbox reportedly picked a price that simply felt reasonable; optimizing it came later, once the stakes were high. Price is a feature you A/B test, not a vow. **You can always grandfather early users** — today's users are a tiny fraction of the users you'll have in 6–12 months.
- **The three-seconds test:** watch their face after you say the price.
- **Prepay over transactional** (a one-off per session ties price too tightly to your time). 
- **Show tiers and prices on the landing page from the start** (three service packages with real prices).
- **Free-model rules.** Free is legitimate only as: (1) *open core* (adoption → enterprise support/upsell), (2) *structured freemium* — a constraint people actually want to lift (users, messages, storage, seats), launched as the upgrade, with tracked free→paid conversion *and a time frame* ("we expect an upgrade within two weeks when they need another seat"), (3) *huge ad-supported consumer* — and be honest that's the model. Warning sign: building ever more surface area without ever charging more. At launch, freemium is a marketing tactic, not a business model. If you have thousands of free users and nobody pays, study the few who *do*; they probably want something different.
- **Free pilots:** a pilot is evidence only if the champion spent political or financial capital. "Apple is a customer" — "how much do they pay?" — "nothing."
- **Solve the money problem; don't punt it.** (Airbnb could have made payments free because handling money was awkward — the right move was to solve payments well, because money is part of the product.)
- **Price for services with a "free session → pilot → paid" ladder:** free assessment/pitch → 5–10 free with skin in the game → once 10+ people really want it, charge → next 10 at half off.

## 4. Unit economics (do the math; don't poll opinions)

"There are two kinds of questions you shouldn't ask: ones that can be solved with a spreadsheet, and ones that can be solved with testing."

- **LTV, CAC, LTV:CAC, payback, gross margin.** Use them to decide which product or channel gets resources. A $1,000 live test per candidate market beats a $250k study.
- **Leverage per unit of time:** 1:1 tutoring at $60/h × 4h = $240/day vs. 1:8 small groups at $35 each = $280/h × 4h = **$1,120/day** (~4.5× per hour), and each student pays *less* than 1:1. Semi-private beats 1:1 when you're not yet good enough for unlimited.
- **Labor arbitrage once the model works:** get great at (1) signing students up and (2) templatizing the delivery; hire delivery at ~$50/h against $280/h of class revenue; keep **service margins at ≥80%**; use your freed time to sell and recruit. Create a supply pipeline by asking every provider "any friends who'd love to make twice as much in half the time?"
- **Speed-to-lead economics:** a $60k person whose only job is to call every new lead within 60 seconds can pay for himself many times over (55% lead conversion in one example).
- **A model that only works at the founder's skill level is broken.** "You should be able to have people close half as many as you and still make the model work."
- **Lifetime gross profit per customer** is one of three strategic buckets (with *more customers* and *less risk*). The 4th R — **resell/upsell** — is where much of it comes from.
- **Sanity numbers:** a service business needs enough clients to fill the schedule ("50 students" at 3 sessions/week); a partner offer of free non-cash rewards can beat a 10% kickback on a cheap product.

## 5. Vehicle fit — the Idiot Index

"Your business vehicle matters more than your effort or intelligence." (Buffett's smarter, harder-working classmate went into steel and earned far less. The difference was the boat.) Ask: **what business vehicle gives me the best chance of reaching the outcome I want?** And always: **what does it look like maxed out — do any billionaires come from it?**

| Vehicle | Traits | Examples | Use for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Labor-heavy commodity** (hard, low money) | low barrier, fierce competition, capped, people needed for incremental gain | sit-down restaurants, service-based gyms, SMB agencies, in-person retail, event planning | avoid if you want to scale |
| **2. Easy / low money** (starter cash) | simple, one skilled person can reach a top-1% income; capped | drop-shipping ($1–2M/yr possible), power-washing/lawn, vending/ATMs, wholesaling, Airbnb arbitrage, affiliate content | cash flow, skills, capital — not a long-term big vehicle |
| **3. Medium / medium — expert-dependent** | high margin, small team, "you in an iron-man suit"; a job, not an asset; hard to sell; survivorship bias ("how many Joe Rogans?") | online coaching, AI implementation, construction, rehab, surgeons, enterprise agencies, personality brands | high income |
| **4. Hard / high money — moat-built** | barriers shrink competition: deep skill, network effects, long compounding, regulation, capital | true AI (not wrappers), pharma, specialty manufacturing/chips, marketplaces, franchising (needs non-talent-dependent unit economics), roll-ups, finance, banks, energy/telecom, SaaS (moat eroding as AI lowers skill bars) | enterprise value, billions |
| **5. Easy / high money — supply–demand arbitrage** (the promised land) | demand tailwind or supply constraint, often a **window in time**; regulation; sticky/mandated demand; compounding fees | med spas, wealth advisory, elder care, regulatory-change booms (Botox, cannabis, GLP-1), real-estate syndication, commercial cleaning (sticky; the hard part is getting customers), fire-safety inspection, funeral services, elevator maintenance, debt collection | when you can find the window |

Heuristics: difficulty can be an advantage ("if everyone can do it, everyone will do it" — better to compete with 20–50 players than 5 million, if you can raise money/hire/handle compliance); regulation is a green flag *if you play by the rules*; businesses that "don't sound cool at a cocktail party" have fewer entrants; a restaurant and a software company take the same 60 hours a week, but only one can be a billion-dollar company.

**Goal matching:** high income → expert-dependent; cash flow → easy; enterprise value → scalable, transferable, defensible; billions → massive market + moat. Choose the boat, then row as hard as you can.

## 6. Which customer game? (first-customer fit)

Selling software to startups is legitimate only if you're playing a known game:
- **The Stripe game:** customers at two people and an idea; they never outgrow you (payments scale with activity); you ride them to IPO.
- **The Gusto game:** startups as a proving ground; then the broader SMB market because the software is similar.
- **The AWS game (bottoms-up):** engineers adopt it at startups, carry it into big companies, you train a whole population — but you still end up building enterprise sales.
- **Not a game:** "sell to big companies by first selling to people who have nothing in common with big companies." If the product is only useful at 500 engineers or massive data (Snowflake, Workday-style), the natural customer is the big one — long sales cycle or not. "The only thing worse than a long sales cycle is selling to someone who doesn't have the problem yet."

## 7. Funding path

**Default: bootstrap the first business** to ~10 paying customers *before* deciding whether you need money at all ("your first $1,000 in revenue teaches you more than $2M in funding"). Customers validate *your* model; investors validate *their* thesis, teach you to pitch, and push you toward exit rather than customer value. Your **first business** should usually be bootstrapped to "pay off ignorance debt" — don't lose friends-and-family money because you don't know what you're doing. A bootstrapped product that takes off can still raise later.

**Four paths (Hormozi's 2×2, whose money × whose business):** bootstrap (your money, your business) · raise capital (their money, your business) · invest (your money, others' businesses) · fund management (their money, their businesses — GP/LP leverage). Pick one and play it for about a decade; don't chase shortcuts; don't copy an outlier's path without their starting conditions.

**When VC fits (YC):** it invests where the outcome could be worth **100–1000×**; if you can't explain why you might IPO and return a lot, you can't raise it. It exists for businesses needing millions upfront to break even, where no other mechanism supplies it (Google couldn't buy servers with a loan). The vast majority of businesses should not raise VC (the funded share is single-digit % or less). A "no" is nothing personal. Raise out of an internal desire, "not from YouTube." And note: nobody has built a trillion-dollar software company without VC.
- **Capital-heavy/winner-take-all** (platforms, networks, marketplaces, manufacturing, pharma) → outside money may be the only way. Costs: two masters (customers *and* investors), dilution, terms, board control (founders can be voted out), and 1000× math that is fine for a fund but is 100% of your life.
- **Two company types:** *venture-scale* (huge rounds, exit on investors' timeline) vs. *durable, profitable* (owned, steady, founder-paid). Both valid. You cannot take a $5M check from a fund built around outlier returns and later decide to run a profitable business at your own pace.
- **A great small business is a win.** A product making ~$30–50k/month with ~10 hours of upkeep "won."

**If you do raise — stage-appropriate proof** (figures from a 2025–26 source; re-verify):
- Pre-seed: idea + maybe prototype; pitch = who you are and what you'll build; mostly SAFEs (~92%), caps ~ $10–15M, many rounds <$250k.
- Seed: working product + early users; median post-money ~$24M (late 2025), AI premium on top.
- Series A: revenue and growth, how you'll spend; now ~ $2–3M ARR (the old rule was $1M); AI Series A valuations ~38% higher than non-AI.
- Series B: revenue in the tens of millions; "the less it's about your story, the more it's about the number on your dashboard."
- Match pitch and investor to stage; regular startups must now prove paying customers, retention and a believable next milestone.

**Dilution:** investors don't buy your shares; the company prints new ones. 50/50 co-founders, company issues 20 new shares to 100 existing → 50/120 = 41%. "Your share count doesn't change; your percentage does."
**Valuation vs. terms:** valuation is the headline; terms decide control. $5M at $25M with worse terms can be worse than $5M at $18M. Ask "what did I give up to get it?" Know four terms: **liquidation preference** (1× non-participating is founder-friendly; participating = money back *plus* a share of the rest), **protective provisions** (investor vetoes), **pro-rata rights**, **drag-along**. They're written for the bad moments (down round, bridge, low offer, missed milestones, board conflict).
**Runway:** track it live; target 24–30 months at a raise; running out between rounds means a bridge (worse terms) or death. Founders get "surprised slowly" by scattered spend.
**Don't benchmark against the hottest AI rounds.**
**Investor-pitch honesty:** show the high probability and the risk mitigations; never say "guaranteed." Founders who know every unit economic inspire conviction. Delete the exit-strategy slide at idea stage.

## 8. Partner & channel offers (if distribution is partner-led)
Treat the partner as a new customer; start with the offer. Currency: money, free stuff, or both (non-cash often beats a 10% kickback and avoids 1099 paperwork). Tier it: scarce/unscalable top prize (in-person dinner), mid, long-tail; make the little guy able to win. Win-win-win: you, the partner, and their audience. See `features-mechanics.md` for the full machine.

## 9. Spending & equity hygiene (see `plan-check.md`)
Don't pay advisors or PR in equity; ask would-be advisors to *invest* instead; negotiate legal payment plans; customize nothing you don't need.

## 10. Red flags in a money model
Free pilots as proof · a $0 logo · LOIs collected to raise while usage is nil · "we'll figure out pricing later" · price set from your cost · freemium with no constraint/tracking · raising VC for a business that can't return 100× · a services business sold as software · a founder-dependent model · a capped vehicle for a "billion-dollar" goal · enterprise tool sold to startups at $100/month.

