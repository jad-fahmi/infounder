# launch — getting the first customers and users

Goal: a concrete **14-day plan** to land the first 10 — with the exact person, place, script, daily volume, free-pilot terms, and a pass/fail. First-10 work is manual, personal and a little embarrassing on purpose; that's how you learn before you automate.

---

## 0. The mindset

- **It's a search problem, not a persuasion problem.** Most people are not early adopters. (How many products do you use that you were among the first 10 users of? For most people: zero.) Find the few who love trying new things or have a burning need — the "Gustafs" who bring a startup's product into their company. Their size and reputation don't matter; their problem does.
- **Study them like an anthropologist who found a hidden civilization:** how do they decide, why would they trust *you*, what do they want?
- **Launch early** to give the right people a wide surface area to find you.
- **Charge real money early.** Early adopters aren't price-sensitive; paying customers give sharper feedback.
- **Experiment constantly** on pricing, landing page, onboarding, features — and talk to users. Don't fear churn or an annoyed first user: early relationships are personal and repairable; "when you run a bad experiment, no one writes about it. You're fighting irrelevance, not headlines."
- **Early buyers buy the founder.** They buy because they trust you, not just because the product is great. So be a person.
- **Drop the shame.** "If you want to start a business you should probably get rid of your shame, because you're going to get embarrassed. Ask lots of people."
- **Do you like your users?** (YC's weirdest, best question.) Caring is a competitive advantage big companies can't copy: "It's really hard to beat a competitor who cares about the customer more than you do — the customer knows in their bones."

## 1. Fast setup (do this before any outreach)

### a) Pick your who — "the room test"
Three buckets: **(1) people like you**, (2) people you've already helped, (3) people you think are underserved. Default to (1): you know their pains, demographics and hopes, so you skip research — and you become a missionary. **Narrow with at least 3 of 5 frames:** age, gender, profession, problems/pains, interests/passions. *"If you could fill a room with one type of person you'd be excited to help, what do they do, what do they care about, what are they suffering from?"* (e.g. "35-year-old male accountants bored at work".)

### b) Answer the buyer-habits questions (≈1 hour)
Where does the target customer actually spend their time? What does their average day look like? How often do they check email? Do they go to conferences — which? Are they on Reddit? LinkedIn? Do they pick up the phone? Do they ask friends for recommendations? Which newsletters do they read? If you can't answer concretely, you haven't spent enough time with real customers — fix that first. **Pick the channel where the buyer lives, not where outreach is convenient.** (Cold email is easy, laptop-based and has tooling — and fails for school administrators, property managers, insurance agents and truck dispatchers. One founder closed more at a trade-show floor in 3 days than in 3 months of cold email.)

### c) Write the one-liner
*I help [who] get [outcome] without [dread].* ("shoot — that's me.") For a quick offer: "I created a product that helps [target customer] solve [problem]. Would you like to try?" — clean, no buzzwords.

### d) Decide what's free and what's paid
See "Free pilot terms" below. Default is paid; free only with skin in the game.

### e) Findability plumbing (if you have a website; 1-2 hours, once)
Not a growth strategy and too slow to be your only channel pre-PMF, but free to set up. Verify the site in **Google Search Console** and **submit the XML sitemap**; give every page a unique title and meta description and one H1; make key pages answer real customer questions clearly (good for classic **SEO** and for being cited by AI answer engines: **GEO**); add schema.org markup where it fits; and if you serve a local or service-area market, claim and complete your **Google Business Profile**. Checklist in `ai-era.md` §4. Then go back to outreach: the first ten customers come from conversations, not rankings.

## 2. The customer-number ladder

| Customers | Source | How |
|---|---|---|
| **1–3** | Personal network: friends in the industry, ex-colleagues, classmates, one intro away | Ask directly. "Basically no counter-examples." They buy you, not the product. |
| **4–10** | Things that don't scale | Fly out. Reddit/forum DMs. Small dinners. Personalized LinkedIn DMs. Free consulting sessions. In person. Tedious and manual — "a really good way to learn before you automate" — and it signals you care enough to spend your own time. |
| **10–50** | Now you have a message worth scaling | Apollo, Clay, email sequences, content. Prospecting tools only matter after **10–20 quality customers**. |

**Warm-lead order:** (1) personal network, (2) **second-degree connections** (browse who your connections know — one founder got intros to about half her customers this way), (3) AI network-search tools. **Intro-ask rule:** say exactly who you want to meet, why they'd care, and what the forwarded note should say — make it effortless for the introducer.

## 3. Tactics (pick two, not twelve)

### In person
Closing in the room beats almost anything. Persistence stories: flying to one buyer four weeks in a row; showing up uninvited and getting asked to leave most times; a customer thrown out after eight minutes who became a top account.
- **Small, industry-specific conferences:** put 15-minute Calendly slots back-to-back across every day; email the attendee list a couple of times beforehand to fill slots; email again during the event to catch non-openers.
- **Micro-events:** a founder dinner or happy hour for the ICP — 6–10 people, $50–100 a head. Once someone has eaten dinner with you they rarely ignore your email, and sometimes they go out of their way to help.
- **Go where the cohort gathers:** e.g. sports teams for athletes who need a test score; after-practice groups spread word fast.
- **Scrappy event tactics** (when you must be at a big event): a memorable stunt tied to your product (an ice block with money frozen inside); slip flyers under hotel doors when the event bought all the rooms; get a quiet room and take calls. "Approach events like a scrappy startup founder"; a $20k side-room talk nobody attends is worse.

### Online communities where pain is public
Reddit, Facebook groups, Discord, YouTube comments, industry forums, trade-association boards. Find old threads about your exact problem and DM every commenter, one by one. One healthcare founder made replying to complaints her job for a couple of months (2–5 posts a day). Threads are indexed by Google and persist for years, so the work keeps paying. A bad launch post can still send hundreds of quiet lurkers to sign up.

### Warm outreach (Hormozi's simplest system)
1. Greet. 2. Mention something **specific and complimentary** about them. 3. Insert your one-sentence offer. 4. Ask if they know anyone who could benefit.
**Volume rule:** do it ~4 hours/day, or until you've reached 100 people — "if you do this every day you will get five customers." Volume beats cleverness: 300 flyers is not a test; 150,000 is. Call new leads within **60 seconds** (4–5× conversion). Do *more*, then *better*, then *new*.

### The Core Four (any channel is one of these)
Post content · run ads · outreach to strangers · outreach to people you know. Start with warm outreach + a compelling offer (short term), content (long term). Allocate roughly 70/20/10 (core/adjacent/moonshot). Ads: use small budgets to learn messaging; don't let them become your growth engine (a company ran ~$1M/month of ads profitably for years, then it stopped working all at once; an owned product loop would've been cheaper in year two than year six).

### Outbound (when you've earned it)
- **Apollo:** lead database + email finder + sequencer; enough for most first lists.
- **Clay:** research/enrichment with AI (what software they use, who's hiring, who just posted about X).
- **LinkedIn Premium:** best fresh professional data; send the connection request **without a message**, then a short DM once accepted.
- **Copy rules:** under **75 words**; one clear call to action (reply? call? 15-minute demo?); read it aloud to a friend and rewrite anything you wouldn't say to a person; **give before asking** (20 minutes of work before asking for 30 minutes of their time); follow up 3–4 times over a couple of weeks. The test: "If I received this email, would I reply?" — founders usually answer "absolutely not."

### Frame it as something other than a pitch — but only if it's real
Ask for advice, mentorship, a product review, a whiteboard session. Don't disguise a sales call as advice-seeking if you're not genuinely open to learning. Examples that worked: asking dozens of CEOs to be a mentor (a few became customers); **interviewing 200 salespeople before building** — maxing LinkedIn connections each week around one hypothesis (~50% accepted, ~20% of those took calls), "user research on steroids," ending with a pipeline of people who'd told her exactly what they needed; offering startups free whiteboarding on their agent architecture (which happened to require the product); **paying lawyers $100–200/hour for product feedback** (~30% accepted; works in high-contract-value markets).

### Public challenge: Show HN (https://news.ycombinator.com/show)
Put the working product in front of engineers and founders who will tear it apart, so the assumptions get challenged by strangers before investors, or the market, do it for you. It is a stress test first and a distribution channel second.
- **Fits when:** dev tools, technical or infra products, B2B software with a technical or founder buyer, anything the user can show working today. **Skip when:** consumer apps, non-technical buyers, or no demo yet. Do the Demo → Sell → Build step first.
- **Requirements:** something people can try right now with no signup wall or waitlist (a Show HN of a landing page gets ignored); a title that says plainly what it does; a first comment from the founder with the backstory, how it works, and **the specific assumptions you want challenged** ("I think X is true. Tell me where I'm wrong.").
- **Ask for the attack:** the useful replies are "this already exists", "why not just use X", "who pays for this", and "this breaks when…". Log them verbatim like the "no"s in the 14-day plan, then sort into *answered by evidence*, *answered by changing the product*, and *real hole*.
- **Read it right:** HN readers are often not the buyer. Praise and upvotes are not validation, and a front-page spike with zero paying users is a vanity win. The signals that count are objections you couldn't answer, and anyone who asks "can I pay for this" or "can I use this at work".
- **Rules:** never ask friends to upvote or coordinate votes (HN penalizes it). Stay in the thread and reply to every substantive comment for several hours. Don't get defensive. Take the harshest comment seriously first.
- **Order:** after at least a few real conversations with target customers, not instead of them. Use it to sharpen the pitch and the moat story before fundraising.
- **Pair with** the competitor/positioning check in `roast.md`: that finds the gap on paper, and Show HN finds out whether it holds up in public.

### Partner / affiliate distribution
Build the machine, not just a link: offer → page → promotion → onboarding/activation → collateral → comms → human follow-up for the top 50–100 (full spec in `features-mechanics.md`).

## 4. Customer interview script (when you're not the customer)

Run it like a sales call. Ask:
1. What problems are you struggling with right now?
2. How long have you struggled with them?
3. What makes it painful, specifically?
4. Describe what it's like when it's not right.
5. Describe your ideal experience if it were done the right way.
6. What do you have to *stop doing* that you like doing — and don't want to stop?
7. What do you have to *start doing* that you hate doing?
Answers 1–5 + 6–7 populate the good-stuff and bad-stuff lists for the offer. Stay behavioral: past facts ("what did you do last time?"), not hypotheticals.

Other probes: "How much do you pay today to deal with this?" · "What did you try?" · "Who else should I talk to?" · "If I built this, would you pre-order it?" (the only version of "would you buy it" worth asking).

## 5. Free pilot terms (when you must give before you get paid)

1. **Free session = half assessment, half sales pitch.** Show one easy win, then: "this is how I work — do you like that?"
2. **Give the first 5–10 free with skin in the game:** "If you don't do your homework, I'll bill you."
3. **Take the Big Three in exchange:** feedback, a testimonial, a referral.
4. **Start charging once ~10 people really want it.** Post testimonials as content. People who engage get a DM with a better offer. The next ~10 clients at half off.
5. **Prefer prepay** over one-off transactions.
6. **Free is evidence only if there's commitment.** A free pilot with a non-invested champion proves little.

---

## 6. Demo → Sell → Build (when you can't ship yet)

Make a demo (mock, video, clickable prototype, fake door) → try to **sell** it (get a commitment, ideally money) → only then **build**. Most sales don't need a working product, just a good demo. "If someone won't buy your demo, they definitely won't buy your product." The objection "we're different" is always wrong. Where an AI-built MVP is cheaper than a demo, build the MVP in hours and go straight to 10 users.

**The 90-day blueprint** (Maurya): in 90 days you can (1) design a starting business model, (2) validate demand *before* building, (3) go all in if it checks out. Cap planning at 90 days. Bootstrap to 10 paying customers; then decide if you need funding.

**The Dropbox move:** a three-minute demo video of a product that doesn't exist yet, shown to the right audience, to gauge desire.

## 7. Growth after 10: the happy-customer loop

Forget growth hacks; there's one engine:
1. **Activation** — get users to the aha moment fast.
2. **Retention** — make them repeatedly happy.
3. **Referral** — make it easy (and rewarding) to tell others.
The happy-customer moment is "the only causal step that matters." Optimize it before acquisition. Then: Four Rs — retain, review, refer, resell. Customer-success tactics: time messages around high-posting moments; reduce friction to invite; incentivize.

Brand angle: peers trust peers more than marketing. Invite customers to co-author; spotlight real moments; scarcity and identity build demand; patience compounds.

## 8. The 14-day plan template

```
## First-10 plan
**ICP room:** <3+ traits> · **Where they are:** <specific places> · **Buyer-habits answers:** …
**One-liner:** I help … get … without …
**Offer & price:** … (free pilot terms if any)
**Channel (and why this one):** … · **Backup:** …
**Daily volume:** <N contacts/day> for 14 days (≈ <N×14> total)
**Script:** <exact message, ≤75 words, one CTA, a specific compliment>
**Tracking:** contacted → replied → call → committed → paid (one sheet)
**Day 3 review:** reply rate? If <X%, change the message, not the channel.
**Day 7 review:** calls booked? If <Y, change the channel.
**Day 14 pass/fail:** PASS = ≥3 paying or ≥10 committed pilots; FAIL = <1 committed → revisit who/pain/offer.
**What I learn from the "no"s:** log every objection verbatim.
```
(The pass/fail numbers are suggested defaults of infounder's own, not sourced figures — set X and Y to the user's reality; the point is to pre-commit to thresholds *before* you see the results.)

## Anti-patterns
Tooling before talking to anyone · optimizing subject lines before confirming the channel fits the buyer · an MVP + self-serve funnel + hope · building a user base from the people who are easiest to reach rather than the people who most need it · tiny test samples · a Show HN with no working demo, or one treated as demand proof · free with nothing back · mass-emailing templated notes with one customized word · begging · pretending to be a big company · forgetting to ask for the referral.
