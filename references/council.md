# The council — consult the best operators, then decide

Load this for `ask` mode, or any time the user is in doubt and wants more than one framework. The goal: when someone asks infounder what to do, the answer should be what they'd get from a table of great founders and operators, **compressed into one decision**: each seat's actual instinct, where they truly disagree, a ruling, and a test.

Design principle (from the source material): the people who win don't copy their heroes' *outputs*; they absorb how their heroes *decide* and apply it to their own context ("models, not mentors"). So each seat below is a **decision pattern**: what they believe, the question they always ask, what they'd push, and where their advice breaks.

## Ground rules (honesty)

1. **Lenses, not ventriloquism.** Write "Altman lens: …", never "Sam Altman says: …" and never invent quotes. A lens is a reconstruction of a person's public decision pattern. It is not their words, and they have not endorsed infounder.
2. **Source tags.** `[T]` = grounded in the transcripts in this project's source material. `[P]` = public record from general knowledge; paraphrased and approximate; verify before quoting or relying on specifics. If you are not confident about a seat's view on a topic, say "no confident read" and move on. Don't fill the gap.
3. **Quotes.** Put a person's *words* in quotation marks only for lines in "Safe short quotes" at the bottom. Everything else is paraphrase. (Named concepts and labels such as "models, not mentors" or "Day 1" may appear in quotes; they're terms, not attributed speech.)
4. **Anecdotes are illustrative.** Company stories are told to make a principle concrete. Mark numbers approximate.
5. **Seats are priors, not verdicts.** Every seat has a blind spot. Naming it is half the value.
6. **People not on the council.** If asked "what would X do?" for someone not listed, say whether you have a confident read from their public record; if not, say so and offer the closest seat.

## How to run the council

1. **Classify the doubt.** idea · product · customers · money/pricing · funding · team/hiring · focus/pivot · timing/launch · competition/moat · fear/psychology · scaling/control.
2. **Seat 3 to 4 advisors** (up to 6 only for a big, irreversible decision or if the user says "full council/board"). Use the stage table below. **Always include one seat that would argue the other side.**
3. **One or two lines per seat**, concrete to *this* situation (their repo, numbers, words), not a generic recital of the person's philosophy.
4. **Find the real disagreement** (there usually is one) and say what it hinges on.
5. **Rule.** Decide using, in order: (a) **stage** (pre-PMF → learn/sell; post-PMF → systematize); (b) **the founder's goal** (income / cash flow / asset / billions); (c) **reversibility** (two-way door → decide fast on ~70% of the info; one-way door → slow down, pre-mortem); (d) **the evidence ladder** (what they've done and been paid, not what they say).
6. **Close like a co-founder:** *what would change my mind*, **Test it** (numeric pass/fail), **Next 48 hours**.

Output shape is in `SKILL.md` (mode `ask`). Keep it to about one screen.

## The seats

### Sam Altman lens  `[T]` + `[P]`
- **Core bets:** Wait for a genuinely good, *original* idea; don't cast around and "make something up." Most people copy, and clones lose to whoever started first (a thousand social networks after Facebook, a thousand photo apps after Instagram; the year one fusion startup launched, ~10,000 photo startups did). Notice a **technology wave** early, while others call it a toy, and surf it; great companies cluster right after a wave starts (internet mid/late 90s, mobile after 2007). Problems from your own life are the highest-hit source. Persist through iteration: "100 bricks" thrown together eventually look like a building; most people quit with too few iterations. Prefer 2-3 co-founders; never add one to please investors; never stop looking. Step one before step two (his own path ran through a normal startup). Ambition is under-supplied.
- **Always asks:** What's the wave and are you early? Is this an idea only you'd start, or one a thousand others are cloning? How many *real* iterations have you done? What would you do if you were 10× more ambitious?
- **Would push:** pick the original angle; commit and iterate; ride the model/tech curve instead of fighting it.
- **Blind spot:** Silicon-Valley-scale ambition applied to a beginner with no step-one assets; "wait for a good idea" becomes procrastination. Pair with Seibel.

### Paul Graham lens  `[P]`
- **Core bets:** *Make something people want.* **Do things that don't scale**: recruit users by hand (Stripe's founders reportedly set up a new user's account on the spot; Airbnb's founders went door to door in New York to fix listing photos). **Startup = growth**: a weekly growth rate is the heartbeat (roughly 5-7%/week is good, 10% exceptional, for the right stage). Live in the future and build what's missing. **Schlep blindness**: the best ideas hide behind annoying work (Stripe). **Default alive or default dead?** Better to have a small number of users who love you than a lot who merely like you.
- **Always asks:** Who'd be upset if this disappeared? What's your weekly growth? Are you default alive? What's the schlep everyone's avoiding?
- **Blind spot:** growth-startup lens. Many great businesses aren't growth startups; weekly growth needs a user base to measure. Use it from first users on, not at the idea stage.

### Michael Seibel & Dalton Caldwell lens (YC operators)  `[T]`
- **Core bets:** Do you *like* your users, and have you talked to them this week? Charge early: the best feedback you'll get is in the **three seconds after you say the price**. Launch to find out; "turn the water on" instead of inspecting every pipe. Don't think like an investor (TAM, comps, exit slide) at idea stage. Hire late, spend little: PR agencies, ads, events and execs are pre-PMF traps. Don't copy your idols' end state; most "mere mortals" must do step one. If you *bring* expertise, trust your gut; if you're *building* it, stay small and curious. Bootstrap unless the business is capital-heavy and 100× capable; only add a co-founder because you need one, never because investors like it.
- **Always asks:** What did users say last week? What's the price? What's the smallest thing you can launch this week? Is that your job, or are you hiding from selling?
- **Would push:** ship now, sell now, talk to users, spend nothing.
- **Blind spot:** software-venture lens; underweights vehicle choice and low-tech businesses where a small shop is the right answer.

### Mark Zuckerberg lens  `[P]` + `[T]`
- **Core bets:**
  - **Start and let the idea form.** Ideas don't arrive fully formed; they clarify as you build. Facemash became Facebook became Meta `[T]`; your hypothesis is "almost certainly wrong from the jump" and the job is to correct it fast. Speed of learning is the edge.
  - **Ship, measure, repeat.** Bias to action; code (working product) wins arguments; done beats perfect; focus on impact. (Facebook's early values included *move fast*, *be bold*, *focus on impact*.) Not breaking the core path matters more once it's critical infrastructure; they later reframed "move fast" to include stability.
  - **Find the one activation number.** When people don't stick, find the early behavior that predicts retention and rebuild onboarding around it. The popular telling is that new users who connected with enough friends within the first days stayed; the exact numbers vary by telling `[T]` (the transcript's version is about 10 friends in 14 days), but the method is the point: make the product get new users to that moment, fast.
  - **Win one dense network, then expand.** Facebook opened school by school, so each new campus launched into an already-dense graph.
  - **Platform paranoia.** He publicly called betting on HTML5 over native mobile a major mistake and then moved the company mobile-first; he bought Instagram and WhatsApp when they looked like the next shift. Ask who owns the next platform; neutralize or join it early. (Counterweight: he also turned down early buyout offers, e.g. Yahoo in 2006.)
  - **Stay the technical founder.** Hands-on with the product; high hiring bar (reportedly, he'd only hire someone to report to him if he'd work for that person; treat as approximate).
- **Always asks:** What's the one number that proves people stick? How fast can you get a new user there? What's the next platform shift and which side are you on? Can you ship it this week? Who's the real competitor in three years?
- **Would push:** ship ugly, instrument the activation moment, go deep in one tight community first, watch the platform.
- **Blind spot:** scale-platform lens. "Move fast and break things" and growth-first can burn trust in safety-, privacy- or money-critical products (protect the core path); copy-or-buy-the-threat is not available to a two-person team. Weight his advice on *speed and measurement*, not on tactics that need a billion users.

### Jeff Bezos lens  `[P]`
- **Core bets:** Obsess over the customer, not the competitor; customers are perpetually, productively dissatisfied. **Work backwards**: write the customer-facing announcement and FAQ before building. **One-way vs two-way doors**: reversible decisions should be made fast by a small group with ~70% of the information; irreversible ones deserve slow, careful thought. **Disagree and commit.** Stay in "Day 1" (resist proxies, slow decisions, process as a substitute for results). Think long: be willing to be misunderstood for years. Build a **flywheel** (lower cost → lower prices → more customers → more volume → lower cost). Regret minimization: which choice will you regret at 80?
- **Always asks:** What does the customer say on day one? Is this a one-way or two-way door? What's the flywheel? What won't change in ten years that you can build on?
- **Blind spot:** the heavy working-backwards machinery is for scale; pre-PMF take the *principle* (start from the customer, decide reversibles fast), not the document ritual. Optimizing for long-term at the expense of runway kills small companies.

### Steve Jobs lens  `[T]` + `[P]`
- **Core bets:** **Focus is saying no**, to hundreds of good ideas; be proud of what you didn't ship. **One compelling point, communicated compellingly** ("a thousand songs in your pocket"; the "Catch" exercise: one ball you can catch, four you can't). Delete everything that sucks about the category and what remains is the product. Start from the customer experience and work backwards to the technology. **A players hire A players**, and finding them is the founder's work (he'd spend a year or more recruiting someone). Perfect the few details you keep.
- **Always asks:** What's the one sentence a stranger can catch? What are you saying no to? Would you be proud to ship this?
- **Blind spot:** taste and polish before there are users is a trap; the bar assumes you already have a customer and resources. Use "subtract" and "one thing" now; use "perfect every detail" after the core is proven.

### Elon Musk lens  `[P]` + `[T]`
- **Core bets:** Reason from first principles (what's the physical/cost floor?). His five-step "algorithm": **question every requirement** (attach a name to it), **delete** the part or process, **simplify/optimize** what remains, **speed up** cycle time, **automate last**. Don't optimize what shouldn't exist. Unreasonable public deadlines as a forcing function (Hormozi cites this `[T]`). Fail fast on purpose; if nothing is failing you're not pushing. Vertical integration where the supplier margin is the opportunity. He frames running a company as staring into the abyss and chewing glass `[T]`: you inherit the worst, unsolved problems.
- **Always asks:** What does it cost in raw inputs; why is the product 10× that? Which requirement can you delete? What if you had a quarter of the time?
- **Blind spot:** capital and talent advantage, survivorship bias, intensity as a substitute for validation. A solo founder with no budget can't "just work harder" past a wrong premise. (Naming note: Musk's "idiot index" is cost of finished product ÷ raw materials; Hormozi uses the same words for a different thing, a vehicle-choice chart, in `monetization.md`.)

### Alex Hormozi lens  `[T]`
- **Core bets:** **Offer** first: "I help [specific who] get [dream outcome] without [thing they dread]"; add a named unique mechanism. **Volume with feedback**: do 100 reps, study the top 10%, repeat (his mentor's flyer test size was 5,000, not 300; mistaking low volume for volatility). **Vehicle > effort** (the boat, not the rowing). **Commitment is the elimination of alternatives**; pick one thing ("niche-slapping"). **Leverage** (code, media, other people's time). Learn from people ahead in *models, not mentors*. Charge; narrow = pricier. Hire a barbell (young hungry + seasoned), document-demonstrate-duplicate, buy back your time in order, and "know where the bodies are buried" in each department. Sell the outcome, not the vehicle ("the hole, not the drill").
- **Always asks:** Who exactly, what outcome, how fast, how certain, what do they *not* have to do? How many reps have you done? What's the one thing? What does this look like maxed out, and does anyone get rich from it?
- **Blind spot:** service, info and SMB bias; "volume and discipline" can mask a broken offer; a lot of his advice assumes you're already selling.

### Ash Maurya lens  `[T]`
- **Core bets:** The business model matters more than the idea. **Demo → sell → build.** Measure what customers *do*, never what they say ("will you pre-order?"). Hand-pick your first ten early adopters and charge from day one; freemium is a tactic, not a model. Revenue teaches more than funding. Date a co-founder on a three-month project. Build a better *photographer*, not a better camera (the outcome lives in a bigger context). 90 days is enough to test a model. The only growth engine is the **happy-customer loop**: activation → retention → referral.
- **Always asks:** Will they pre-order? What's your unfair advantage? What's the real problem and what do they pay today? What does the customer become by using it?
- **Blind spot:** can turn into validation theatre; some products (deep tech, hardware, trust-critical) need a build to demo.

### Peter Thiel lens  `[P]`
- **Core bets:** Competition destroys margins; aim for a **small market you can dominate**, then expand (Facebook began as a single campus network). Ask the **contrarian question**: what important truth do few people agree with you on? Seven checks: engineering, timing, monopoly, people, distribution, durability, secret. Durability (will you still matter in ten years?) matters as much as growth.
- **Always asks:** What's your secret? Can you be the only one in a small market within a year? Why does this last?
- **Blind spot:** contrarianism for its own sake; monopoly framing too early is the investor-thinking Seibel warns against. Use it once you have a wedge to defend.

### Warren Buffett & Charlie Munger lens  `[T]` + `[P]`
- **Core bets:** The **vehicle** beats the effort (Buffett's smarter, harder-working classmate chose steel `[T]`). Stay inside your **circle of competence**. Look for a **moat** that's widening, not eroding. **Invert**: how would I destroy this business? Avoid stupid mistakes before chasing brilliant ones. Compounding needs time; patience is the edge. Be honest in the hard moments.
- **Always asks:** Is the moat widening or eroding? What would make this fail? Is this inside your circle? Does anyone get rich from the maxed-out version?
- **Blind spot:** investor lens. A builder learns the circle by working in it; waiting for certainty means never starting.

### Brian Chesky lens  `[T]` + `[P]`
- **Core bets:** **Founder mode**: the founder stays close to the details and to customers, and doesn't delegate the product's soul. Do things that don't scale: Chesky emailed hosts, flew to New York, visited apartments, and found the listing photos were the problem `[T]`. Design the **11-star experience** (the absurdly great version) and back down to what you can do by hand for ten customers. Build something a hundred people love before something a million kind of like (advice he credits to Graham). Ideas investors dismissed (strangers sleeping in your home) can be the big ones. (Early cash came partly from selling novelty cereal boxes.)
- **Always asks:** What does the 11-star version look like, and which piece can you do by hand this week? What did you learn standing in your customer's home?
- **Blind spot:** founder mode can be micromanagement; post-PMF the answer is delegation with visibility (Hormozi's buyback ladder).

### Patrick & John Collison (Stripe) lens  `[P]`
- **Core bets:** Obsess over the developer/user's first ten minutes and reduce integration to the smallest possible effort; install it for them by hand at first; care about craft; write clearly; be patient about quality. Sell to customers who grow with you (the "Stripe game" `[T]`: they start at two people and stay through IPO).
- **Always asks:** What's the fastest path to first value? Can you do the setup yourself for the first twenty customers? Who grows into bigger spend?
- **Blind spot:** infrastructure/developer-tool lens; the craft bar can delay shipping.

### Terence Reilly (Stanley, Crocs) lens  `[T]`
- **Core bets:** A brand is **stewarded, not owned**; its meaning is co-created. Invite creators and customers to co-author the story and they'll take you places you can't buy. Be a **peer marketing engine**, not a megaphone. Use **scarcity and limited editions** to show demand. Seize the cultural moment (a viral clip of a cup surviving a car fire became a free cup and a free car). Build the brand slowly on a good product; Stanley was a 110-year-old brand, not a venture-forced sprint.
- **Always asks:** Who co-authors your story? What can customers make with your product that you never could? Where's the community, and does it signal identity?
- **Blind spot:** needs a product people already like plus some audience; scarcity can backfire (the Target scrums), and a safety/quality question must be answered fast and openly.

### Bench (one line each; call when the topic fits)
- **Marc Andreessen** `[P]`: product/market fit is the thing that matters; you can feel when it's not there. Use for "are we PMF?".
- **Reid Hoffman** `[P]`: if you're not embarrassed by the first version, you launched too late. Use for launch timing; do *not* use for trust-critical products.
- **Stewart Butterfield** `[P]`: Slack came out of a failed game; the "we don't sell saddles" memo says sell the change in how people work, not the object. Use for pivots and positioning.
- **Jensen Huang** `[P]`: long-horizon technical bets made before demand was visible (CUDA); stay alive until the wave arrives; he owns a single-digit % of Nvidia `[T]`. Use for "wave" and "moat".
- **Jack Dorsey** `[T]`: perfect every detail and limit the number of details. **Frank Slootman** `[T]`: if the CEO can't name the one thing, the CEO failed.
- **Keith Rabois** `[T]`: a few "barrels" limit throughput; more ammunition doesn't fire faster. Hire barrels.
- **Sara Blakely** `[T]`: solve a problem you personally have (Spanx). **Drew Houston** `[T]`: a three-minute demo video *was* the MVP for Dropbox.
- **Marty Neumeier** `[T]`: a brand is a customer's gut feeling, not a logo; you're creating millions of brands, one per head.
- **Jane Wurwand** `[T]`: to reach your 20% you must be willing to annoy the 80%; stand for something and you stand against something.
- **Naval Ravikant** `[P]`: specific knowledge + leverage (code and media are permissionless leverage); play long-term games with long-term people.

## Where the seats genuinely disagree (and the usual ruling)

| Tension | Who | Ruling |
|---|---|---|
| Wait for a great idea vs. start now | Altman/Thiel vs. Seibel/Zuckerberg | Idea comes from your life and conviction, then gets tested by action. Don't gate on perfect; don't start from nothing. Time-box exploration inside one space. |
| Small wedge vs. huge ambition | Thiel/Seibel vs. Altman/Musk | Specific wedge, ambitious end state. Win one dense niche first. |
| Move fast vs. protect trust | Zuckerberg/Hoffman vs. Jobs/Bezos | Fast on reversible, polish and safety on the irreversible and the core path. |
| Polish vs. launch | Jobs vs. Seibel/Hoffman | Launch the core; perfect the few details you keep. No polish before users. |
| Founder-in-the-weeds vs. delegate | Chesky/Jobs vs. Hormozi | Pre-PMF: do it yourself. Post-PMF: delegate and keep visibility ("where the bodies are buried"). |
| Raise vs. bootstrap | Altman/Thiel (capital-heavy) vs. Seibel/Maurya/Hormozi | Bootstrap to ~10 paying customers; raise only if capital-heavy, winner-take-all and 100×-capable. |
| Compete vs. avoid competition | Seibel (fine) vs. Thiel (escape it) | Competitors prove demand; you still need a wedge with an insight they lack. |
| Customer obsession vs. vision | Bezos vs. Jobs | Obsess over the *problem* and outcome; don't build every feature requested. |
| Volume of reps vs. getting the premise right | Hormozi vs. Maurya | Do both: small cheap test first (demo/sell), then volume on what works. |

- *"Move fast and break things."* / *"Done is better than perfect."* (widely cited Facebook-era mottos)
- *"It's always Day 1."* / *"Disagree and commit."* (Bezos shareholder letters)
- *"Focus is about saying no."* (Jobs, paraphrase-close; treat the gist as the point)

## Voice (how the council should sound)
- Operator, not analyst: *"Monday I'd email 20 support leads and ask for a $49 pre-order"*, not *"consider validating willingness to pay"*.
- Concrete: names, numbers, scripts, file paths, the user's own words.
- One real disagreement beats five polite agreements.
- Warm and a little irreverent; disagree out loud; praise only what's earned; no hype, no hedge piles.
- Never end on "it depends." End on the call, what would change it, the test, and the next 48 hours.
